03 / How the evaluation works

What did we test?
And what didn't we?

The point of this exercise is disciplined judgement. Instead of presenting a polished metric and declaring victory, we expose the rules, alternatives and unknowns behind an operating decision.

01

Create two fictional claims populations

A deterministic random generator produces 2,600 claims per scenario. Amount, history signal, an unusual-history flag and a routine/complex-care pathway affect the simulation's retrospective label and score.

02

Apply one scoring rule and decision cutoff

The scores are generated by an illustrative nonlinear formula; no model is trained or independently validated in this page. The 0–1 score is not shown to be a calibrated probability. A flagged claim is routed to human review.

03

Count correct decisions and mistakes

Every threshold generates true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives. Accuracy, recall, precision and flag rate are calculated from those counts, including comparisons across the two invented pathways.

04

Change conditions and examine the consequences

The shift scenario deliberately increases the complex-case share and changes how the simulated score relates to review needs. Capacity and costs are then calculated using transparent hypothetical assumptions.

05

Separate a teaching demonstration from a deployment gate

Passing these toy checks would still not establish deployment readiness. Real assurance requires representative data, prospective testing, measured calibration, uncertainty, security, case review protocols, monitoring and appropriate governance.

04 / A necessary boundary

A convincing demonstration
is not the same as evidence
of production safety.

What this proves: a threshold change can alter detection, error burden, review volume, subgroup behaviour and hypothetical costs. The interactions actually recalculate all those outcomes from generated case-level data.

What it does not prove: that any deployed claims model has these characteristics, that the scores are calibrated, that the fictional labels are clinically valid, or that the simplified cost weights reflect actual member harm or organisational losses.

What a responsible team would do next: scrutinise the target definition; examine leakage, label quality, subgroup sample sizes and uncertainty; run independent temporal and prospective validation; document human override and appeal procedures; monitor shift, errors and downstream outcomes; and establish accountable deployment criteria.

Intelligence is not just about prediction.
It is about responsibility.
