The uncomfortable truth about data-driven decision making

“Data-driven” sounds reassuringly rigorous. At its best, it is. At its worst, it becomes a polished way of giving numbers more authority than they have actually earned.

The promise of data-driven decision making is attractive for obvious reasons. Gather enough information, organise it well, and uncertainty begins to feel more manageable. Decisions can become more disciplined, assumptions can be tested, and weak intuition can be challenged by patterns that are visible in the evidence.

But that promise is often overstated. In practice, data can create the appearance of certainty without fully delivering the substance of it. A dashboard can make incomplete information feel complete. A metric can feel persuasive because it is precise. Correlation can begin to travel as if it were explanation. And people can start saying “the data shows” when what they really mean is “this is the part we measured.”

The problem is not data itself. The problem is how data behaves once it enters organisational life. Numbers gain authority quickly, especially when they are packaged into clean charts and regular reporting rhythms. They can appear more settled than they really are. Definitions narrow. Missing context disappears from view. Assumptions built quietly into the model are forgotten, while the output continues to circulate as if it were neutral and complete.

This is why mature teams ask harder questions than “what does the dashboard say?” They ask what was left out, what assumptions are shaping the result, what remains difficult to measure, and whether frontline experience supports the pattern being reported. They ask where uncertainty is large enough to change the decision, and where a neat metric may be hiding a messier reality.

For that reason, I prefer the phrase data-informed with judgment to the more fashionable slogan. It leaves room for context, qualitative evidence, institutional memory, and strategic humility. It recognises that good analysis sharpens thinking, but does not remove the need for interpretation.

None of this is an argument against data. It is an argument against naivety. The aim is not to become suspicious of evidence, but to become more careful about what evidence can and cannot do on its own. Good decision intelligence does more than display information. It helps an organisation distinguish between what it knows, what it infers, and what it still needs to understand.
